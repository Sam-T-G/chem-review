# Where the chemistry comes from

Every rule and number the app shows, with its source. No citations go on screen: the grounding lives here, in code comments and in the tests (D-0004). A new fact gets a line here and a test in the same change.

## Sources

- **Element data** (`src/chem/elements.data.json`) is generated by `scripts/gen-elements.mjs` from Valence's validated `data/elements.json` (group, period, and Pauling electronegativity from PubChem). Nothing in it is typed in by hand.
- **Valence and inner-shell electrons** follow OpenStax Chemistry 2e, section 6.4: valence electrons are the ones in the outermost shell, and the rest are core electrons. The shell picture draws the full inner shells (2, 8, 18, 18) and the valence electrons, and a test checks that each atom's shells add up to its atomic number.
- **Lewis structure rules** follow OpenStax Chemistry 2e, sections 7.3 and 7.4: count valence electrons (add for anions, subtract for cations), least electronegative atom in the middle, hydrogen never in the middle, octets where possible, formal charge to pick between drawings, and the three kinds of exception (odd-electron, electron-deficient, hypervalent).
- **Shapes** follow section 7.6: the five electron-group arrangements, their ideal angles, the names for arrangements with lone pairs, lone pairs in equatorial seats of a trigonal bipyramid, and two lone pairs on opposite sides of an octahedron. The group count follows the same section: a single, double or triple bond is one group, and a shape is named for where the atoms are.
- **Angles a row holds besides its label** (`alsoAngles` in `src/chem/vsepr.ts`): the table gives one label per row ("90° and 120°", "90°"). Two atoms in the 2 axial positions of a trigonal bipyramid, or on opposite corners of an octahedron, are also 180° apart. This follows from the positions section 7.6 describes; it is worked out from the geometry and is not a number quoted from the book. The quiz and the exercises never offer one of these angles as a wrong answer. `tests/vsepr.test.ts` holds the list written by hand and checks it against the ideal positions.
- **Measured angles** are shown for two molecules only, both printed in section 7.6: water 104.5° and ammonia 106.8°.
- **Bond polarity and molecular polarity** follow Sam's Chem 1A lecture slides for chapter 7 (summer 2026, on Interactive General Chemistry, Macmillan Learning; D-0021). The slides are on Sam's machine and not in the repo. From them: ΔEN is the larger electronegativity minus the smaller; 0 to 0.4 is a nonpolar bond, above 0.4 up to 2.0 is polar covalent, above 2.0 is ionic; the less electronegative atom is δ+ and the more electronegative is δ−; a bond dipole is an arrow that points at the more electronegative atom, and a nonpolar bond gets none; bond dipoles can cancel in a linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral or square planar shape when all the outer atoms are the same. The slides' worked examples are the expected answers in `tests/polarity.test.ts`: HCl (3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, polar), CO2 (3.5 − 2.5 = 1.0, the dipoles cancel), H2O (3.5 − 2.1 = 1.4, polar), NH3 (0.9, polar), CH2F2 (C–F 1.5 polar, C–H 0.4 nonpolar, a polar molecule), SF6 (nonpolar), nitrate (the dipoles cancel) and ozone (ΔEN is 0, and the formal charges still make it polar).
- **Sigma and pi bonds, and hybridization** follow the chapter 8 slides: the first bond between two atoms is always a sigma bond, and a second or third is a pi bond; 2 electron domains is sp, 3 is sp2, 4 is sp3; a double bond is still one domain, because its pi bond is not hybridized. The slides name sp3d and sp3d2 as the other types without giving their domain counts. The app uses them for 5 and 6 domains, as OpenStax Chemistry 2e, section 8.2, does (PCl5 and SF4 are sp3d, SF6 is sp3d2).
- **The key terms' cards** (D-0027, D-0028) take their definitions from the key terms of OpenStax Chemistry 2e and from the course slides. From chapter 2: atomic number ("number of protons in the nucleus of an atom"), group ("vertical column of the periodic table"), period ("horizontal row") and ion ("electrically charged atom or molecule"). From chapter 6: valence electrons ("electrons in the high energy outer shell(s) of an atom") and core electrons. From chapter 7: covalent bond, single, double and triple bond, lone pair ("two (a pair of) valence electrons that are not used to form a covalent bond"), Lewis structure, octet rule, formal charge, resonance, electronegativity ("tendency of an atom to attract electrons in a bond to itself"), polar covalent bond, bond dipole moment, polar molecule and bond angle ("angle between any two covalent bonds that share a common atom"). From chapter 8: sigma bond, pi bond and hybridization.
  - Section 7.2: electronegativity "increases from left to right across a period" and "decreases down a group", fluorine is the most electronegative (4.0), and the more electronegative atom has the partial negative charge.
  - Section 7.4: formal charge is the valence electrons, minus the nonbonding electrons, minus the number of bonds; the formal charges add up to the charge; the smallest formal charges are preferable; a molecule "does not fluctuate between resonance forms", and its structure is the average of them.
  - Section 7.6: lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs; "Molecular structure describes the location of the atoms, not the electrons"; a single, double or triple bond is one region of electron density; a dipole arrow points at the more electronegative atom, with a small plus sign at the other end.
  - The slides: the octet and duet rules, boron with 6 electrons, atoms from the third period on holding 10 or 12 electrons as central atoms to lower formal charges, an electron domain as "a charge cloud made up of either shared or lone-pair electrons", sigma bonds from head-on overlap and pi bonds from p orbitals side to side, "Pi bonds are weaker than sigma bonds", and hybrid orbitals as equal-energy orbitals for bonds and lone pairs.
  - The cards' 26 example molecules (`SAMPLES` in `src/modules/lewis/guide.ts`) are not guided problems. Their Lewis structures, shapes, polarity and bond counts are worked by hand in `tests/terms.test.ts` with the rules above and the course's electronegativity table. Hydrogen chloride (ΔEN 0.9), ozone and the nitrate ion are the slides' own examples. Boron trichloride, phosphorus pentafluoride and the shapes of sulfur tetrafluoride and chlorine trifluoride are named in section 7.6, and the other shape examples (selenium tetrafluoride, bromine trifluoride, iodine pentafluoride, the tetrachloroiodate ion) are the same counts of bonded atoms and lone pairs with another element.
  - One sentence goes past its sources: the resonance card says the bonds of its example (ozone, or the nitrate ion) are all the same, each in between a single and a double bond. Section 7.4 says this of the nitrite ion and says a structure is always the average of its resonance forms. The slides give ozone and nitrate as examples of resonance. No page that was read states it for those two.

### The exercise pool (D-0032)

`src/chem/exercises.ts` holds 43 molecules. Their answers are worked by hand in `tests/exercises.test.ts` with the rules above and nothing else: electron counts from the group numbers and the charge (7.3), drawings by full outer shells, formal charge and D-0005 (7.3, 7.4), both geometries and the bond angle text from section 7.6, ΔEN and polarity from the course table and the slides (D-0021), and resonance as more than one best drawing under D-0005.

- The class worksheet (Sam's photos, 2026-11-17, D-0030) gives the 20 molecules and the columns asked for. The handwritten answers on it are a student's and are not used. Its one printed row, water, matches the app: 8 electrons, tetrahedral, bent, less than 109.5°, O–H 1.4, polar.
- Electron geometry is the arrangement of all the electron groups on the middle atom, lone pairs included, and molecular geometry is the arrangement of the atoms (section 7.6: "Molecular structure describes the location of the atoms, not the electrons"). `Problem.geometry` holds the first and `Problem.shape` the second.
- Two atoms (IF, HF, HCl, HBr, F2, Cl2, Br2, N2, O2) always sit on a line, so the molecular geometry is linear and there is no bond angle. The one bond's dipole is the molecule's net dipole. HCl's 0.9 is the slides' own example.
- The app also answers linear for the electron geometry of a two-atom molecule, because it has no middle atom. No section that was read says this. By the definition above (all electron groups, lone pairs included), a course that treats one atom as the middle atom would count 4 groups on iodine in IF (one bond, three lone pairs) and answer tetrahedral. - CH3+ leaves carbon at 6: 4 + 3 − 1 = 6 electrons, all in the three C–H bonds, and hydrogen takes no double bond. - Expanded middle atoms beyond the guided ones (SiCl6 2−, SeCl4, ICl5, ICl2−, ICl3, BrF4−, AsF5, ClF5) are the same counts of bonded atoms and lone pairs as section 7.6's examples (PCl5, SF4, ClF3, XeF2, SF6, BrF5, XeF4, IF5) with another element.

## Conventions

### Expanded octets (D-0005)

Formal charge decides between drawings, and an atom from period 3 onward may take more than 8 electrons to make the formal charges smaller. So sulfur dioxide is drawn with two double bonds, sulfate with two, phosphate with one. A drawing that keeps sulfur at 8 with charges of +1 and −1 is sent back with a hint.

The textbook's text shows expanded shells only where the electron count forces them (PCl5, SF6, IF5, XeF4). If a course wants sulfur kept at 8, the change is `DEFAULT_CONVENTION` in `src/chem/solve.ts` to `'octet'`, and dropping the three problems in the "Expanded octets with double bonds" group. That is Sam's decision.

### Two electronegativity tables (D-0021)

The app holds two sets of electronegativities, and each has one job.

- The Pauling values in `src/chem/elements.data.json` decide which of two atoms is more electronegative when the middle atom is picked. No number from that table is printed.
- The slides' rounded table (`COURSE_EN` in `src/chem/polarity.ts`) is the one a student subtracts with in step 6, so the numbers on screen match the ones the course works by hand: H 2.1, Be 1.5, B 2.0, C 2.5, N 3.0, O 3.5, F 4.0, Al 1.5, Si 1.8, P 2.1, S 2.5, Cl 3.0, As 2.0, Se 2.4, Br 2.8, Kr 3.0, I 2.5, Xe 2.6.

A test checks that each rounded value is within 0.2 of the Pauling value, and that the two tables agree on which atom is δ− for every bond in the guided problems. One bond lands on a different side of the cutoff: N–O is 0.5 with the rounded table (polar) and 0.40 with the Pauling values (nonpolar). The app follows the rounded table.

### The 3D model's angles (D-0008)

The repulsion model uses made-up strengths (lone pair to lone pair 2.6, lone pair to bond 1.4, bond to bond 1) picked so that the textbook arrangement is the lowest-energy one for all 13 shapes. A test checks that. The strengths are not fitted to measurements, so the app never prints an angle read from the model.

The 3D view marks one bond angle with a dotted arc and a label (two for shapes with two different angles). The label's text comes from the shape table, or from the two measured angles, never from the model. The model only decides whether the arc is drawn: an arc shows while its two bonds really are at that angle, so it goes away while a student pulls the model out of shape. When the outer atoms are different elements (formaldehyde), the label and the panel say "about 120°".

## Known gaps

- In the model's seesaw (SF4) the two middle bonds settle about 125° apart, wider than 120° where the textbook says narrower. The "less than 120°" arc is not drawn for that pair, and the panel still gives both angles in words. Fixing it means retuning the three strengths against the 13-shape test (W-006).
- A net dipole that a student draws is judged against the same sum, taken with each bond's direction as the viewer sees it (`netAsSeen` in `src/chem/polarity.ts`). Perspective is ignored. The directions the tests expect (water's from the hydrogens toward oxygen, sulfur dioxide's from sulfur toward the oxygens, formaldehyde's from carbon toward oxygen) are worked by hand from the slides' rule that each arrow points at the more electronegative atom.
- The net dipole is the sum of the bond dipoles in the textbook arrangement, each as long as its ΔEN. Lone pairs add nothing of their own and bond lengths are ignored, as on the slides. That gets polar or nonpolar right for all twenty guided molecules (`tests/polarity.test.ts` lists them), but the arrow's length is not a measured dipole moment, and the app prints no number for it.
- Free build applies the same table and the same cut-offs to any bond on the board (D-0029). Its one case beyond the guided problems is an ionic bond: Al–F, 4.0 − 1.5 = 2.5, above 2.0, so ionic (`tests/guide.test.ts`).
- With five or six electron groups and outer atoms that are not all the same (PCl3F2), polar or nonpolar depends on which corners the atoms take. Free build says so and gives no answer.
- CH3− and PH2− follow the app's rules literally, and a course key may say otherwise [TBD: Sam to confirm against his instructor's key]. CH3−: C–H is 0.4, nonpolar on the course table, so there are no bond dipoles and the ion comes out nonpolar, though it is trigonal pyramidal with a lone pair. PH2−: P–H is 2.1 − 2.1 = 0.0, nonpolar, so it also comes out nonpolar.
- The slides give the formal-charge rule for a ΔEN of 0 for ozone only. `moleculePolarity` applies it only to a bond between two atoms of the same element, so P–H, C–S, C–I and N–Cl (equal table values, different elements) get no arrow whatever the formal charges. It shares the formal charges of like outer atoms equally before sizing the arrows, as the slides' "−1 shared by the outer two" says, so ozone's two arrows are the same length and its net dipole runs along the bisector, from the middle oxygen toward the outer two, for either drawing (`tests/polarity.test.ts`).
- Some answers rest on a convention where courses differ, and a course key may differ from the app [TBD: Sam's instructor's key settles each]:
  - ΔEN exactly on a cutoff. C–H is 0.4, which the slides call nonpolar (CH2Cl2, CHCl3, CH3−, CH3+). B–F and As–F are 2.0, which the slides call polar, not ionic.
  - SO2 and SO3 are drawn with sulfur past 8 under D-0005, so the app gives them one drawing each and no resonance. A course that keeps sulfur at 8 draws resonance forms.
  - Polar or nonpolar is asked of ions (CH3−, PH2−, H3O+, NH2−), where a course may only ask it of neutral molecules.
  - The ideal angles (109.5°, 120°) are given for molecules whose outer atoms are not all alike (CH2Cl2, CHCl3, COCl2), where the real angles differ a little.
  - ICl5 is on the worksheet and in the pool, although it is not a known compound. Its answers are worked the same way as BrF5's.
- O2 is drawn O=O with every electron paired, as a Lewis structure draws it. Its two unpaired electrons are outside what a Lewis structure shows.
- For two atoms, `ShapeInfo.groups` and `lonePairs` describe one atom (the one that is not hydrogen, or else the less electronegative one). They do not set the geometry, and the hybridization worked from `groups` is that atom's.
- Molecules with more than one middle atom (ethane, hydrogen peroxide) can be built in free build but have no reference answer and no 3D shape. Odd-electron molecules (NO, NO2) have no reference answer. There are no typed-in formulas (W-007).
